3Tokens: what the model actually reads

A model can't read letters or words. Your text is first chopped into tokens, and each token is swapped for a number. Everything after this point is math on those numbers. Different models chop text differently.

4Context: what is really sent

The model has no memory between requests. Every turn, the app re-sends the rules plus the whole conversation so far. That bundle is the context, and it has a size limit, so longer chats cost more and eventually don't fit.

Why this is only an estimate
A real model assigns a probability to every token in its vocabulary at every step, then samples one. Here we only see what comes back from repeated sampling, so rare options may not show up. Temperature 0 makes the model always take its top choice, so you'll see a single bar. Higher temperature spreads the choices out.

  







  

    
Tools and agents

    
A model can only produce text. To do something it asks the app to run a tool, then reads the result. A model that keeps looping like this (think, use a tool, read the result, think again) is what people call an agent. Grounding: it only knows what's in the window

    
The model has never seen your company's documents. Ask about an internal policy and it produces the most plausible-sounding answer, which may simply be invented. That's a hallucination, and it's the same prediction you saw in the next-word lab. Paste the real document into the context and the answer changes. Apps that look up documents and paste them in for you are doing exactly this; it's called RAG (retrieval-augmented generation).

1Oversharing: the AI finds what was already exposed

    
Company assistants search internal documents and paste what they find into the context. If the search doesn't check who is asking, the assistant happily answers from documents that person was never meant to see. Nothing was hacked. The permissions were already wrong; the AI just found them faster.

2Hidden isn't secret: the system prompt leaks

    
Builders sometimes put things in the system prompt they assume users can't see: discount codes, internal URLs, even keys. But the system prompt is just more text in the context, and the model can be talked into repeating it. A refusal most of the time still means a leak some of the time. Run it ten times and count.

    
    
    

    
    
    

      
      
      
      
      
    

    

    

  


  

    
3Denial of wallet: tokens are a bill

    
Every request costs money per token, in and out. A public chatbot with no limits, a script hammering it, or an agent stuck in a loop turns into a surprise invoice. Token caps and rate limits are security controls, not just budget controls. 1Embeddings: how AI search finds "similar"

    
Before an assistant can paste a document into the context, it has to find the right one. Most systems turn each piece of text into a long list of numbers (an embedding) where texts with similar meaning land close together, even when they share no words. This runs a small open embedding model right here in your browser, not Claude. The first run downloads it (about 25 MB).

    
    
    

    

    

    
Talking point: "firewall blocked the traffic" and "wall caught fire" share the most letters, but land far apart. "Blocked" and "dropped at the perimeter" share no words but land close.

3Leading questions: it tends to agree with you

    
Models are trained to be helpful, and that can tilt into telling you what your question implies you want to hear. Ask the same thing neutrally and with a built-in assumption, five times each. A second model call then grades each answer (this is called LLM-as-judge, and the judge is imperfect too).

4Testing something that never answers the same way twice

    
You can't write a normal unit test for an AI feature, because the output changes every run. Teams measure instead: run the same prompt many times, check each answer against rules, and report a pass rate. "Works on my machine" becomes "passes 92% of the time".
